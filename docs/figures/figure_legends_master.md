## Detailed Figure Explanations

## F001 – Longitudinal Infection Status Heatmap

**What it shows:**
A heatmap of all participants (y-axis) across timepoints (x-axis), where each cell represents the infection status of an episode.

**Why it’s important:**
It provides a high-level overview of the cohort's longitudinal data, showing the prevalence of UTI vs. ASB and the completeness of sampling.

**How to read it:**
- **X-axis:** Timepoints (T0, T1, etc.).
- **Y-axis:** Individual participants.
- **Colours:** **UTI** is Orange, **ASB** is Blue, **Negative** is Grey.
- Look for rows with alternating colours to spot phenotype switching.

**Key takeaway:**
Most participants show stable phenotypes (mostly ASB or mostly Negative), but a subset exhibits switching between ASB and UTI.

---

## F002 – Infection Status Transitions

**What it shows:**
A flow diagram (or heatmap) quantifying transitions between infection states from one timepoint to the next (e.g., T0 -> T1).

**Why it’s important:**
It reveals the stability of ASB and UTI. Do ASB cases progress to UTI? Do UTIs resolve to Negative?

**How to read it:**
- **From Status:** The status at the earlier timepoint.
- **To Status:** The status at the subsequent timepoint.
- **Colours:** Indicate the starting status (**UTI**=Orange, **ASB**=Blue).

**Key takeaway:**
ASB is highly stable; transitions from ASB to symptomatic UTI are relatively rare in this cohort.

---

## F005 – Top 40 Virulence Factor Genes

**What it shows:**
A bar chart ranking the 40 most common virulence factor (VF) genes found in the *E. coli* isolates.

**Why it’s important:**
It identifies the "core" virulence repertoire of this nursing home population.

**How to read it:**
- **Y-axis:** Gene name.
- **X-axis:** Percentage of isolates carrying the gene.
- **Bars:** Longer bars mean higher prevalence.

**Key takeaway:**
The cohort is dominated by adhesins (e.g., *fimH*) and siderophores (e.g., *fyuA*), typical of UPEC.

---

## F009 – Swimmer Plot (Participant Timelines)

**What it shows:**
Timelines for participants with multiple timepoints, tracking their infection status and strain persistence.

**Why it’s important:**
It visually demonstrates whether recurrent infections are caused by the same strain (persistence) or new strains (replacement).

**How to read it:**
- **X-axis:** Timepoint.
- **Y-axis:** Participant ID.
- **Points:** Coloured by status (**UTI**=Orange, **ASB**=Blue).
- **Lines:** Connect episodes from the same participant.

**Key takeaway:**
Recurrent episodes (both ASB and UTI) are frequently caused by the same persistent strain over months.

---

## F010 – UTI Risk by Sequence Type

**What it shows:**
The probability that an episode caused by a specific Sequence Type (ST) will be symptomatic UTI rather than ASB.

**Why it’s important:**
It tests the hypothesis that certain "high-risk" clones (like ST131) are more likely to cause symptoms.

**How to read it:**
- **X-axis:** Sequence Type (e.g., ST131, ST73).
- **Y-axis:** UTI Risk (Percentage of episodes that are UTI).
- **Colour:** Gradient from Blue (Low Risk/ASB-associated) to Orange (High Risk/UTI-associated).

**Key takeaway:**
Some lineages show higher UTI risk, but common clones like ST131 are often found in ASB, challenging the idea of obligate pathogens.

---

## F011 – Publication Swimmer Plot

**What it shows:**
A polished version of the swimmer plot (F009) focusing on key participants with phenotype switches.

**Why it’s important:**
It serves as the primary visual evidence for within-host phenotype switching (Same strain, ASB -> UTI).

**How to read it:**
- **Orange Triangles:** Symptomatic UTI.
- **Blue Circles:** Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (ASB).
- **Grey Lines:** The same patient over time.

**Key takeaway:**
The same bacterial strain can cause both ASB and UTI in the same patient at different times, suggesting host factors drive symptoms.

---

## F012 – Mutation Map of Switch Isolates

**What it shows:**
The genomic location of mutations (SNPs) identified between ASB and UTI isolates of the **same strain** in the **same patient**. This figure shows **only 2 participants** (40001 and 40004) - the rare "phenotype switches" identified in the cohort.

**Why it's important:**
Despite being rare (N=2), these cases are **scientifically critical** because they test whether bacterial evolution drives the ASB→UTI transition. If the bacteria acquired virulence genes or accumulated many mutations, it would support pathogen-driven symptoms. If not, it points to host factors.

**How to read it:**
- **X-axis:** Position on the bacterial genome (1 to 5 Mb)
- **Y-axis:** Participant ID (only 2 rows: 40001, 40004)
- **Points:** Individual SNPs (Orange = Coding region, Grey = Intergenic)

**Key takeaway:**
**Only 10-12 SNPs** separate ASB and UTI isolates from the same strain. **No virulence gene acquisition** occurred. This reinforces that symptoms are **host-driven** (immune response, catheter status) rather than pathogen-driven (bacterial evolution).

**Statistical note:** While N=2 is small, the finding is robust: both cases show the same pattern (genomic stasis despite phenotype change), and this is consistent across 0-12 SNPs total.
