# Conference Poster Draft: *Escherichia coli* Sequence Type Dynamics in Nursing Home Residents

---

## TITLE
**Within-Host Stability of *Escherichia coli* Sequence Types Among Nursing Home Residents: A Longitudinal Genomic Analysis of Asymptomatic Bacteriuria and Urinary Tract Infections**

## BACKGROUND
*   *Escherichia coli* is the most common cause of both asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) and symptomatic urinary tract infections (UTI) in older adults.
*   Nursing home residents experience high rates of E. coli colonization and recurrent UTI episodes.
*   It remains unclear whether sequential ASB and UTI episodes within the same individual are driven by persistent colonization with a single resident strain or by frequent introduction of novel bacterial lineages.
*   Understanding these within-host dynamics is critical for distinguishing relapse from reinfection and guiding targeted interventions.

## OBJECTIVE
To characterize the longitudinal stability and sequence type (ST) distribution of *E. coli* isolates collected from nursing home residents experiencing repeated episodes of ASB and UTI.

## METHODS
*   **Study Design:** Secondary analysis of the YELLOW RoUTIne cohort (18-month longitudinal follow-up).
*   **Participants:** Nursing home residents with ≥2 timepoints featuring culture-positive *E. coli* bacteriuria.
*   **Microbiology & Genomics:** Isolates underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Sequence Types (STs) were assigned *in silico* using the Achtman MLST scheme. Untypeable isolates were excluded from paired continuity analysis.
*   **Analysis:** We assessed cross-sectional ST diversity and longitudinal persistence (defined as identical STs in consecutive sampling pairs). Only deduplicated, episode-level data (one ST representation per clinical event) were analyzed to prevent inflation.

## RESULTS

**1. Cohort and Isolate Flow**
*   From the primary cohort, 92 participants with ≥2 *E. coli*-positive timepoints were identified, contributing 397 sequenced isolates across 258 clinical episodes with recorded CFU.
*   After episode-level deduplication and exclusion of untypeable isolates (n=70 episodes), the primary analysis subset contained **327 typeable *E. coli* episodes** across 89 participants. *(See Figure 1: Cohort Flow Diagram)*.

**2. Population ST Distribution**
*   The community landscape was diverse, comprising 35 distinct Sequence Types.
*   However, the population was dominated by a few globally disseminated lineages: **ST43 (12.2%), ST6 (9.8%), ST1 (8.6%), and ST4 (8.6%)**. *(See Figure 2: Top ST Distribution)*.

**3. Within-Host Strain Stability**
*   Longitudinal colonization was highly stable. Among consecutive paired clinical episodes (n=68 pairs with typeable neighbor STs), **76.5% (52/68)** maintained the exact same Sequence Type.
*   This high stability was maintained even in the extended follow-up sub-cohort (≥3 timepoints), which demonstrated 73.5% (36/49 pairs) sequential stability. *(See Figure 3: Within-Host ST Stability)*.

**4. Clinical Presentation (ASB vs. UTI)**
*   Of the typeable episodes, 60 were symptomatic UTIs. The ST distribution in UTIs mirrored the overall population but with subtle shifts: ST6 (18.3%), ST87 (11.7%), and ST10 (8.3%) were the most common in symptomatic events. *(See Figure 4: ASB vs UTI Lineage Comparison)*.
*   Illustrative patient trajectories highlight that most individuals maintain stable *E. coli* carriage across months, regardless of whether episodes present asymptomatically or progress to UTI. *(See Figure 5: Participant Trajectories)*.

## CONCLUSIONS
*   **High Endemic Stability:** The majority (~75%) of subsequent *E. coli* bacteriuria episodes in nursing home residents are caused by the same Sequence Type as the preceding episode.
*   **Endogenous Origin:** This persistent colonization suggests that many symptomatic UTIs in this population arise from established resident strains rather than newly introduced exogenous lineages.
*   **Global Lineages:** A small number of globally successful, multi-drug resistant lineages (e.g., ST43, ST131-associated clades) dominate the nursing home reservoir.

## LIMITATIONS
*   Relying on MLST (core genome) provides a conservative estimate of strain switching; higher-resolution typing (e.g., whole-genome SNP analysis) might reveal previously undetected micro-evolution or turnover within the same ST.
*   A substantial proportion of isolates (17.6%) were untypeable by standard *in silico* MLST, potentially censoring specific lineage dynamics.
*   The number of verified symptomatic UTI episodes (n=60 typeable) limited statistical power to formally associate specific STs with transition from ASB to UTI.

## NEXT STEPS
*   Conduct high-resolution SNP-based comparative genomics and pangenome analysis to resolve within-ST strain-level turnover.
*   Characterize the virulence factor (VF) profiles of the persistent lineages vs. transient lineages.
*   Investigate the genomic characteristics of the "untypeable" *E. coli* fraction.
