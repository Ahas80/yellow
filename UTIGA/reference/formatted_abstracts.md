## Abstract 1: Longitudinal Clinical Transitions Between ASB and UTI in Nursing Home Residents (≥2 Timepoints)

**Background**
To better manage urinary tract infections (UTI) in the elderly, it is crucial to understand the natural history of bacteriuria. While asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) is common, its stability and propensity to progress to symptomatic UTI remain debated. We aimed to characterize longitudinal transitions between ASB, UTI, and culture-negative states in a nursing home cohort.

**Methods**
We conducted a prospective longitudinal study of nursing home residents. Urine samples were collected routinely every 3 months for 18 months, plus additional samples during suspected UTI episodes. Samples included voided urine, catheter urine, or urine-saturated incontinence material (worn <12 hours, no visible fecal contamination). Episodes were classified as ASB, UTI, or Negative based on guideline-based clinical criteria. Participants provided $\ge 2$ distinct timepoints. Transitions between consecutive concurrent episodes were analyzed.

**Results**
The cohort included 92 participants contributing 270 evaluable urine episodes. Episodes were classified as ASB (n=213, 78.9%), UTI (n=17, 6.3%), or culture-negative (n=40, 14.8%). Analysis of 178 within-participant transitions revealed distinct patterns. ASB was highly stable; of transitions starting in ASB (n=151), 84.8% (n=128) remained ASB at the subsequent timepoint. Progression from ASB to UTI was observed in 15 instances (9.9% of ASB-start transitions). Transitions from a culture-negative state directly to UTI were rare (n=2).

**Conclusions**
In this longitudinal nursing home cohort, ASB was a stable phenotype that rarely progressed directly to symptomatic UTI. These descriptive findings suggest that ASB may represent a stable host-commensal relationship rather than a precursor to acute infection.

## Abstract 2: Longitudinal *E. coli* Sequence Type Dynamics Across ASB and UTI Episodes in Nursing Home Residents (≥2 Timepoints)

**Background**
*E. coli* is the predominant uropathogen in the elderly, causing both asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) and urinary tract infections (UTI). It is unclear whether specific sequence types (STs) are differentially associated with ASB versus UTI, or if within-host lineage turnover drives clinical status changes. We characterized *E. coli* ST dynamics in a longitudinal nursing home cohort.

**Methods**
We analyzed *E. coli* isolates from a prospective longitudinal study (samples every 3 months for 18 months + suspected UTI). Episodes were defined as ASB or UTI using clinical criteria. *E. coli* isolates were typed using multi-locus sequence typing (MLST). Participants with $\ge 2$ eligible timepoints were included. We compared ST distributions between clinical states and assessed lineage stability across consecutive episodes.

**Results**
MLST data were available for 181 episodes (67.0% of eligible cohort). Overall, 38 distinct STs were identified. The most prevalent lineages were ST- (n=31, 17.1%), ST43 (n=21, 11.6%), ST4 (n=14, 7.7%), ST6 (n=12, 6.6%), ST1 (n=10, 5.5%). In UTI episodes (n=14), 10 distinct STs were found, compared to 33 distinct STs in ASB episodes (n=136). Longitudinal analysis of 96 consecutive isolate pairs from 83 participants showed high lineage stability: STs remained identical in 80.2% (n=77) of consecutive pairs. However, 17 participants (20.5%) exhibited at least one strain switch during follow-up.

**Conclusions**
We observed a diverse *E. coli* population dominated by common globally disseminated lineages. The high within-host stability of STs across timepoints, regardless of clinical status transitions, suggests that clinical symptoms (UTI) may often arise from resident ASB strains rather than introduction of novel lineages. Strain switching was observed but less frequent.
